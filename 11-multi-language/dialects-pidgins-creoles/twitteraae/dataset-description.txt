TwitterAAE — dataset and methodology description (metadata-only prior)
=======================================================================

Source paper: Su Lin Blodgett, Lisa Green, and Brendan O'Connor. "Demographic
Dialectal Variation in Social Media: A Case Study of African-American
English." Proceedings of the 2016 Conference on Empirical Methods in
Natural Language Processing (EMNLP 2016), pages 1119-1130, Austin, Texas.
Association for Computational Linguistics. https://aclweb.org/anthology/D16-1120

Follow-on paper: Su Lin Blodgett, Johnny Tian-Zheng Wei, and Brendan
O'Connor. "Twitter Universal Dependency Parsing for African-American and
Mainstream American English." Proceedings of ACL 2018.
https://aclweb.org/anthology/P18-1131

Home page: http://slanglab.cs.umass.edu/TwitterAAE/
Code (demographic classifier model): https://github.com/slanglab/twitteraae

This document is a description of the dataset and its construction method,
compiled by reading the paper above in full and the project's own site
directly. It contains no tweet text and no tweet identifiers from the
dataset itself; see README.md in this same directory for why.

WHAT THE PAPER SET OUT TO DO
-----------------------------
The paper's own framing, from its abstract: dialectal language is
increasingly present on social media, but few NLP resources exist for
handling it. The authors conduct a case study of African-American English
(AAE) on Twitter, proposing a distantly supervised model to identify
AAE-like language from demographics associated with geo-located messages,
verifying that the identified language follows known AAE linguistic
phenomena, and demonstrating that existing language-identification and
dependency-parsing tools perform substantially worse on this text than on
text associated with white speakers. The paper also released an ensemble
language-identification classifier meant to close that accuracy gap, and
released "a new corpus of tweets containing AAE-like language" — the
TwitterAAE corpus this pocket describes.

WHY GEO-LOCATION AND CENSUS DATA, NOT SELF-REPORTED RACE
-----------------------------------------------------------
Twitter does not collect or expose authors' race or ethnicity, and the
authors explicitly reject inferring it from usernames (citing prior work
using birth-record name statistics for this purpose in non-social-media
contexts) because Twitter display names are frequently not real names.
Instead they use a distant-supervision approach built on geography: a
message's geo-coordinates are matched to the U.S. Census "blockgroup" that
contains them — blockgroups being one of the Census's smallest defined
geographic units, typically 600-3,000 people — and each blockgroup carries
the 2013 American Community Survey's percentages of non-Hispanic white,
non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic (any race), and Asian residents. Each user's
messages are averaged into a length-four demographic vector drawn from the
blockgroups they tweeted from. The authors are explicit that under strong
assumptions this vector could be read as a probability of the author's own
race, but they prefer to describe it more cautiously as a proxy for the
likely demographics of the neighborhood the author tweets from.

THE UNDERLYING TWITTER SAMPLE
-------------------------------
The starting point was a "Gardenhose/Decahose" sample stream archive of
public Twitter messages sent by U.S. authors in 2013 (a random sample of
the full public firehose, not a keyword- or hashtag-based search — the
authors note this distinction matters because keyword-search-based corpora
of AAE-like language, as used by some prior work they cite, systematically
miss AAE speech that does not use the searched-for markers). That archive
held roughly 16 billion messages; about 90 million of those were
geo-located with coordinates matching a U.S. Census blockgroup. After
filtering out likely bot/spam/follower-farming messages (authors with
1,000+ followers, 3+ hashtags, the strings "http"/"follow"/"mention", and
retweets), 59.2 million tweets from 2.8 million users remained as the base
population the demographic model was fit to.

TWO METHODS, ONE SHIPPED
---------------------------
The paper describes two approaches to turning per-user Census-demographic
vectors into a text corpus:

1. An early "seedlist" approach: pick the highest-scoring AA-associated
   terms, then collect tweets from authors who use those terms frequently.
   The authors call this ad hoc (three free thresholds, no principled way
   to set them) and superseded it with:

2. A mixed-membership demographic-language model — the model actually
   shipped and used to build the released corpus. Each of the four
   demographic categories (African-American, Hispanic, Asian, white) is
   associated with its own unigram language model ("topic") over a
   191,873-word vocabulary (every word used by at least 20 distinct
   users). Each user's mixture over these four topics is drawn from a
   Dirichlet distribution centered on that user's Census-derived
   demographic vector, with a concentration parameter (set to 10 in the
   paper, chosen to yield roughly 0.8 posterior-to-Census correlation, not
   tuned against any downstream task score) controlling how tightly a
   user's actual language is pulled toward their neighborhood's Census
   profile versus allowed to diverge from it. Every individual message
   additionally gets its own topic distribution, so a single author can
   write differently in different messages — the paper's own example is a
   tweet by an author in a heavily AA-associated neighborhood that would
   still be inferred as non-AAE-aligned if its actual words are not
   AAE-associated. The model was fit by collapsed Gibbs sampling, 300
   iterations, converging within 100-200; posterior estimates average the
   last 50 samples.

The paper is candid about a limitation this model surfaced: with only two
categories (AA vs. not), Spanish-language terms were absorbed into the AA
topic; expanding to four categories removed that specific confound, but
the "Asian" topic ended up functioning partly as a catch-all for
non-English language generally (its posterior correlates only 0.29 with
Census Asian-population share, versus 0.83-0.87 for the other three
categories). Because of this, the paper states directly that "we only
consider the AA and White categories to be reliable and useful" — a
caveat this repo's own README for this pocket repeats, since it governs
how any downstream reader should weight the Hispanic/Asian labels if they
ever obtain the real data through a legitimate channel.

THE RELEASED CORPUS, AS THE PAPER ITSELF DESCRIBES IT
----------------------------------------------------------
To build an explicit corpus of messages (rather than only model
parameters), the authors collected every tweet from users whose posterior
probability of AA-associated language use was at least 80%, producing an
"AA-aligned" corpus, and did the equivalent for a "white-aligned" corpus.
To reduce non-English-language noise given the uncertainty just described
around the Hispanic/Asian topics, they additionally required each
included user's combined Hispanic+Asian posterior share to be under 5%.
The paper states the resulting sizes directly: "Our two resulting user
corpora contain 830,000 and 7.3 million tweets" (AA-aligned and
white-aligned respectively) — and states its own distribution plan for
them just as directly: "for which we are making their message IDs
available for further research (in conformance with the Twitter API's
Terms of Service)." That sentence — the paper's own words, not this
pocket's inference — is the single strongest piece of evidence behind the
gating decision recorded in this pocket's manifest: the people who built
this corpus judged in 2016 that IDs-only, not raw text, was what Twitter's
terms allowed them to redistribute, and X's terms have only tightened
since.

Note on scale: the pocket brief that commissioned this staging pass
described the corpus informally as "~1.1M tweets." That figure could not
be independently confirmed against the paper (whose own two named corpora
sum to 8.13 million tweets, not 1.1 million) or against the lab site
(which states a zip file size, 5.5 GB, but no tweet count). It may refer
to a different downstream subset, sample, or mirror this pass did not
locate a primary citation for. Rather than resolve the discrepancy by
downloading the gated zip to count records directly, it is left here as an
open, disclosed discrepancy between the pocket's starting note and the
one primary source (the paper) this pass was able to verify a hard number
from.

VALIDATION THE PAPER PERFORMS ON ITS OWN CORPUS
-----------------------------------------------------
Because the authors judged manual inspection of the AA-aligned text
impractical at this scale, they validate the corpus indirectly: comparing
AA- and white-aligned vocabulary against a large standard-English
wordlist (SCOWL, 627,685 entries) and finding markedly higher
out-of-dictionary rates for heavily AA-aligned words (79.1%) than for
heavily white-aligned words (58.2%); and checking that the AA-aligned text
reproduces well-documented AAE phonological and syntactic phenomena from
the sociolinguistics literature, plus internet-specific orthographic
patterns (abbreviations, phonetic shortenings) found by manually browsing
the highest-ranked AA-associated words. The paper also demonstrates, as
one of its central findings, that off-the-shelf language-identification
and dependency-parsing tools of the time performed measurably worse on
AA-aligned text than on white-aligned text — the racial-disparity finding
that motivated the paper's own released ensemble language-ID classifier.
