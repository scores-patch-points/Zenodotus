Dr. Darin Sukha
And so, Dance of the cocoa, as I mentioned last time, is inextricably bound to our culture and
history. It is part of the process that was traditionally done. We have some changes yet now, but
in the context of the historical elements, it served a number of functions. One, the primary function
was to really enhance the physical appearance of the cocoa beans for sale. Beans that looked better
at that time fetched a higher price.
Dr. Darin Sukha

And of course, the way that it was done was by shuffling through the beans and in a rhythmic
motion where they would apply some water or liquid. And it was basically trying to polish the
mucilage on the surface of the beans. But over time, Dancing Cocoa took on a different aspect
because remember, if we look at it historically, the workers who would have engaged in this
activity were of two classes.
One, they were either slaves on the plantation or two indentured laborers. And in both instances,
civil liberties were restricted. And dancing the cocoa became an opportunity and an outlet and a
medium to achieve some social interaction, some passive, aggressive resistance to the social
situation that these people were in. So, the task itself is very tedious. You have to shuffle through
the beans. And it was decided, well, you know what? To make this less tedious, the same way we
listen to music, when we have a tedious job to do, they sing. And, to keep rhythm, to shuffle the
beans, they would bring a drum. Now, remember in those days, drumming was not allowed
because drumming was viewed as a call to assembly associated with unrest. So, this was an
acceptable way to encourage drumming.
And drumming then became part and parcel of the process of dancing the cocoa to establish
rhythm. But it was also a form of silence, not so silent, but also silent protest in that although they
were not allowed to drum to congregate, drumming as part of dancing the cocoa was allowed. So,
it was an outlet for this pent-up frustration that was felt at that time.
Now, it also took on another element. So, the ladies at that time, so the ladies would mainly engage
in dancing the cocoa. And the dresses that were worn at that time were long flowing dresses, not
pants or certainly short skirts, which are worn now. So, they were these long flowing dresses. And
dancing the cocoa actually allowed the young women and the women in general to not flaunt
themselves, but for their own form of personal release and physical expression and emotional
expression as well, right?
So, we had the men dancing, doing their job, drumming, and the ladies, with their long balloon
dresses, shuffling through in a rhythmic motion. So yes, it was also a social space where I'm sure
maybe my grandparents, many, many people's grandparents, found each other during this process.
Yeah? So, dancing the Cocoa really served that function as well, a form of social release, as well
as forming strong social bonds. It also tightened the bonds within the workers who engaged in that
activity through this, the love for what they're doing, the shared suffering that they were engaged
in or undergoing. But love of being together, fellowship and dancing the cocoa became a form of
fellowship.
And if you talk to many people now about dancing the cocoa, they have these very romantic
memories of it. They say, “Oh, yes, as a child, I remember dancing the Cocoa, right? And it was a
freeing experience.”

It was like a grounding experience in many instances. So, I think that's why it should be preserved
as a cultural heritage, right? Now, moving on to the modern side of things, we have done a lot of
research at the Cocoa Research Centre at UWI on how to optimize fermentation and drying.
So, in principle, dancing the cocoa is not something that we would engage in anymore because
there are also food safety elements in the broader understanding of what cocoa qualit y
encompasses now. There are machines that can do that if needed. There are rotary polishers that
do this. So, I think dancing the cocoa is a much more romantic way of achieving the same outcome.
So, in that context, I think it deserves recognition as part of our history and culture and is worthy
of being preserved.
