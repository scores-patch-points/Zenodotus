Anthea Grant
So, tell us a little bit about yourself and your passion for doing this year after year, the cocoa
dancing.
Kerron Eastman
Well, my name is Keron Eastman and I am from Charlotteville, and I am the chairman of the
Charlotteville Heritage Performance for the past two years. And we have been involved in this
presentation for the past 23 years. I think this is our 23rd year and I've been involved since day
one.
Anthea Grant
How did it start?
Kerron Eastman
Well, it was started by - it used to go on before by other people and then it died for a bit. And then
Mrs. Alfred and Annette Alfred came and asked a group of people from the community. You
know, why don't you all come out and try to revive and revitalize the heritage? And, you know,
without hesitation, a number of women, young men, you know, children. We are a group of over
75 people. Because it is not just the people you see present themselves on that day. It takes a lot
of hard work behind the scenes. There are people who will cut wood. They are the people who
will...squeeze the cane, cut the cane and so on who will bake the bread and will provide the wood
for the burning of the you know making of the coal, so it's a lot that goes into natural treasures
they you know every year tell.
Anthea Grant
Tell us about your costume.
Kerron Eastman
Well basically it's just costuming. You know we try to, you know, dress to accentuate, you know,
the cocoa fields and so on. As you know, our fore parents would have gone to the, worked on the

cocoa estate in the early years, and that is what would have sent many of our fore parents and our
parents and grandparents to school by them going and working in the cocoa estate, bringing the
cocoa to the estate, working the cocoa fields, bringing the cocoa, you know, preparing the cocoa
for exporting and you know, the better or what should I say, higher quality as the beans are
processed. You fetch a higher price for the beans and that money is what our fore parents would
have used to educate so many of us.
Anthea Grant
Is cocoa farming in Charlotteville a lucrative thing right now?
Kerron Eastman
Well, it is not that lucrative now. We just do it to keep the culture alive. It takes a lot of work to
do. One of the challenges that we have are pests, parrots, and so on, and squirrels. So, to revitalize
that cocoa industry, it will take a significant amount of funding, and maybe something that is
lucrative that we can take forward.
Anthea Grant
How important is this to you in terms of generations to come?
Kerron Eastman
Well, you see, somebody would have done it before and left it for us. And my responsibility is to
continue doing it and ensure that those who are yet to come or even those who are here can grasp
and get a better understanding of why did our fore parents did it? Why did our fore parents do this?
Why did they pass it on? And why is it important that we pass it on, and it continues from
generation to generation.
Anthea Grant
Is there a technique like in the dance itself?
Kerron Eastman
Well, of course. Yes, of course. You see, what you do when you're dancing the cocoa is that you
polish the beans. And as I said before, when you polish the beans properly, you get a higher, you
fetch a higher price. So, you can't jump on the beans. You can't spread your feet apart. You know,
just keep your foot together and you glide from side to side. And that way you polish the beans
rather than destroying the beans. That would not be good for you.
Anthea Grant
And are there any similarities to the Tobago jig?
Kerron Eastman

Well, basically it's all foot movement, yes.
Anthea Grant
All right. And one last question. What do you think about making the festival a bigger event? I
think it's getting bigger every year, I imagine. But what kind of vision do you have to make it even
bigger than it is right now?
Kerron Eastman
Well, that was a vision that started many years ago, not by me, but by those who would have, you
know, come forward and say, you know, let's do this. And our only role at this time is to ensure
that we continue to play our part and continue to encourage others to come on board and ensure
that it grows. Mind you, this was a very small festival many years ago. No significance at that
time, now thousands of people come to Charlotteville every year, and it is growing every year
bigger and bigger.
Anthea Grant
How proud are you to – I mean, you are at the helm, it’s a lot of hard work. To be from this place,
this rich history, you know, that attracts people from all over the world, as a Tobagonian, as a
native of this village, how proud are you?
Kerron Eastman
You know, it's always a joy to be a part of the arts, as a Calypsonian, as somebody who sings as
well, you know, to ensure that our tourism product is different from many others. And what we
do, you cannot see it or experience it anywhere else. So, you need to come to Charlotteville every
year for Natural Treasures Day.
Anthea Grant
And you said something earlier about cane juice. Is there a link with the sugar cane transitioning
to the cocoa?
Kerron Eastman
Well, not really transitioning, but that was part of what our fore parents would have done. So, there
was sugar cane, and there was also cocoa. So, they both work on the estate that would have
provided them - you know, the tools to process both crops.

Anthea Grant
You know, you said that you were a calypsonian, so I'm going to put you on the spot. Do a little
line of the Cocoa song because we're hearing it, but I want to hear it from the horses’ mouth,

natural acapella style, you know, and how these songs, after you give us a little line, how this song
came about, did it come from the —

Kerron Eastman
What we have done here today is just minuscule to what our fore parents would do. And in order
to keep them active and really enjoy what they do, I believe this song came about because of this,
so that while they dance, they sing and enjoy themselves. So, the song goes like this:
“O tinigo mama tinigo, Wine, wine, tinigo. Come let we dance the cocoa. Wine, wine, tinigo.”
Anthea Grant
Yay! This was great. Thank you so much.
Kerron Eastman
Thank you.
Anthea Grant
I really appreciate it. That's great.
