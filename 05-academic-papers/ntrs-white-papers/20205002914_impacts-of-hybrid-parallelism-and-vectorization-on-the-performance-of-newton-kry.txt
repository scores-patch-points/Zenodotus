     Impacts of Hybrid Parallelism and Vectorization on the
    Performance of Newton-Krylov Methods in Computational
                        Aerodynamics

                  Stephen L. Wood,∗ , W. Kyle Anderson† , Kevin E. Jacobson‡ , and William T. Jones§
                              NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA 23681, USA

             Finding the numerical solution of moderate and high-fidelity aerodynamics problems on
         modern computer architectures involves, 1) decomposing the domain into smaller regions of
         nearly equal size, and 2) allocating computational resources for calculations on each domain and
         communication between domains. Modern computer clusters are composed from hierarchies
         of processing, memory, and communication resources with varying capabilities and latencies.
         This paper focuses on the combination of domain decomposition provided by ParMETIS [1]
         and Newton-Krylov Methods [2–5] for the solution of Computational Aerodynamics problems
         of interest to NASA. Herein, trade-offs encountered when mapping aerodynamics problems to
         modern computer architectures are explored through examples and discussions of trade-offs in
         parallelism from MPI [6], OpenMP [7], and vectorization as partition sizes and computational
         resources are varied. An example of the impact that domain decomposition and MPI+OpenMP
         resource allocation can have on an adjoint calculation is presented in this abstract. The full
         paper will include more detailed examples, discussions of difficulties and potential methods to
         overcome them, and topics identified for future study.


                                                        I. Introduction
    Domain decomposition (DD) was proposed to solve partial differential equations (PDEs) by Schwarz [8] more than
one hundred years ago. Essentially, the DD approach splits a large complicated problem into smaller, simpler sub-
problems, solves and recombines their solutions to provide a global solution [9]. As modern computer architectures have
developed capabilities for concurrent calculations, DD methods have steadily flourished as an important computational
approach to simulate fluid flows [10, 11]. This paper will focus on the combination of domain decomposition provided
by ParMETIS [1] and Newton-Krylov Methods [2–5] for the solution of Computational Aerodynamics problems of
interest to NASA.
    Forming and solving linear subproblems within Newton-Krylov methods can account for more than 50% of wall-time
to solution when utilizing traditional domain decomposition and only MPI for parallelization [5]. Existing linear algebra
packages and vendor libraries strive to be general purpose and are not focused on the antisymmetric sparse linear
systems that arise in Computational Fluid Dynamics, Aeroelasticity, or Aeroacoustics. SPARSKIT [12], MAGMA
[13, 14], PETSc [15], MKL 2019 [16], and CUDA10.2.89 [17], are well regarded numerical packages and their sparse
linear algebra capabilities are widely used in scientific applications. However, these packages each target one or two
computer architectures, do not have compatible software interfaces, and do not provide algorithms capable of hybrid
parallelism. The Sparse Linear Algebra Toolkit [5] (SLAT) has been developed with hybrid-parallel algorithms to meet
the needs of Computational Aerodynamics software developers their end users. In this study, the algorithms within
SLAT are used to explore the trade-offs of hybrid parallelism and vectorization on modern computational architectures.


                                                  II. Numerical Methods

A. FUN3D+SFE
     FUN3D+SFE is a continuous finite-element discretization within FUN3D [3, 18]. The discretization is a stabilized
finite-element approach based on the Streamlined Upwind Petrov-Galerkin (SUPG) scheme [19, 20]. For turbulent flows,

the SA-neg-QCR2000 [21, 22] is tightly coupled with the mean flow equations, yielding a nonlinear algebraic system
of equations with six variables at each grid point. A linear nodal basis is used in this study, which is designed to be
second-order accurate in space. The current implementation includes capabilities for computing on tetrahedra, hexahedra,
pyramids, and prisms. FUN3D+SFE is used to perform forward CFD analysis as well as adjoint analysis [23, 24], which
is required for goal-oriented adaptation. Forward CFD analysis and adjoint analysis use real-values in double (64 bit)
precision, to represent the domain, initial conditions, and solution [3].
     To advance the solution toward a steady state, the density, velocities, temperature, and the turbulence working
variable are updated in a tightly-coupled Newton-Krylov-type solver described in Ref. [3]. Here, an initial update to
the flow variables is computed using a locally varying pseudotime-step parameter that is multiplied by the current
Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) number, which is adjusted during the iterative process. During each iteration of the
nonlinear solver, SLAT is used to solve the linear system using a Generalized Minimal Residual (GMRES) method
right-preconditioned with an Incomplete Lower-Upper approximate solve [25]. The steady-state RANS solutions in this
study have been obtained by reducing the mean flow and turbulence-model residuals at least nine orders of magnitude,
thereby ensuring that the magnitude of all residual is below 5 × 10−10 .

                                                         III. Results
     For goal-oriented adaptation of the NASA Juncture Flow (JF) model [26–28], the approach reported by Loseille et
al. [29] is utilized [30]. This goal-oriented metric only targets the inviscid contributions of estimated error, but has
been used successfully on viscous cases [31]. The inviscid goal-oriented approach involves the optimization of an a
priori error estimate of output functionals for the Euler equations in the continuous grid framework using calculus of
variations, resulting in an analytical formulation for the metric field. The resulting metric field essentially consists of the
sum of Hessians of the Euler fluxes, weighted by the gradient components of the adjoint fields [24]. The adjoint fields
used in the present work were calculated for the drag coefficient output functional as described in Ref. [5]. The resulting
adjoint linear systems were converged at least 13 orders of magnitude to produce costate variable fields used in forming
an Euler adaptation metric.
     Figure 1a shows the relative residual convergence of the drag adjoint linear system for the JF shown in Fig. 1b. Two
decompositions of the 1,720,689 grid point domain over 400 Intel Skylake [32] CPU cores (arranged in 10 compute
nodes) are shown, 1) 400 MPI partitions, and 2) 20 MPI partitions each using 20 OpenMP threads. The traditional
approach of assigning one MPI partition per CPU core does not facilitate significant reduction in the residual of the
adjoint system. The benefit of decomposing the domain into fewer, larger partitions, using the same total number of
CPU cores is clear. Additional details of the Incomplete LU preconditioners and matrix orderings used to study this
case will be presented in the full paper.


                                                        IV. Summary
    The decisions made early in a numerical solution process, to decompose domains and allocate computational
resources, have significant impact on the cost and accuracy of resulting solutions. The Stabilized Finite Element library
within FUN3D and the Sparse Linear Algebra Toolkit are used to examine trade-offs between domain decomposition
and resource allocation approaches. The full paper will include examples and discussion of trade-offs in parallelism
from MPI, OpenMP, and vectorization as partition sizes and computational resources are varied.
